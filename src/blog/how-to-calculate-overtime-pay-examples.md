<h1>How to Calculate Overtime Pay: Step by Step Examples</h1>
<p style="color:#6b7280; font-size:0.9rem;">Published August 9, 2026 · 5 min read</p>

<p>Figuring out your overtime pay should not require a spreadsheet. This guide walks through every common situation with real numbers so you can verify your paycheck or estimate your earnings before the pay period ends.</p>

<h2>The Basic Formula (Most Workers)</h2>

<p>For most hourly employees in the US, overtime kicks in after 40 hours per workweek. The formula is simple.</p>

<p><strong>Overtime rate = Hourly rate x 1.5</strong></p>
<p><strong>Overtime pay = Overtime hours x Overtime rate</strong></p>
<p><strong>Total pay = (Regular hours x Regular rate) + (Overtime hours x Overtime rate)</strong></p>

<h2>Example 1: Standard Hourly Worker</h2>

<p>Maria works in a warehouse and earns $16.50 per hour. Last week she worked 46 hours because of a large shipment. Her regular pay covers the first 40 hours at $16.50, which equals $660. Her overtime rate is $16.50 x 1.5, which is $24.75. The six extra hours at $24.75 add $148.50. Her total gross pay for the week is $808.50.</p>

<h2>Example 2: Bi-Weekly Pay Period</h2>

<p>Some employers pay bi-weekly but track overtime weekly. If you work 50 hours in week one and 38 hours in week two, your overtime calculation is done per week, not averaged across the two weeks. Week one has 10 hours of overtime. Week two has none. The 12-hour shortfall in week two does not cancel out the 10 overtime hours from week one. This is a common payroll error worth checking on your own pay stubs.</p>

<h2>Example 3: Salaried Non-Exempt Employee</h2>

<p>James earns $800 per week and is classified as non-exempt. His employer expects 40 hours per week, making his effective hourly rate $20. If James works 48 hours in a particular week, his overtime rate is $30 per hour. Those eight hours earn $240. His total pay for that week is $1,040 rather than $800. Employers sometimes miss this calculation for salaried workers who fall below the FLSA exemption threshold.</p>

<h2>Example 4: California Daily Overtime</h2>

<p>David works in California and is scheduled for a 10-hour shift. His regular rate is $19 per hour. Federal law would not require any overtime for a 10-hour shift if his weekly total stays under 40 hours. But California law requires overtime pay for any hours beyond 8 in a single workday. David gets 8 hours at $19, which is $152. The remaining 2 hours are overtime at $28.50, adding $57. His daily gross is $209.</p>

<h2>Example 5: Blended Rate for Multiple Jobs</h2>

<p>Some workers hold two positions with the same employer at different rates. If you earn $15 per hour stocking shelves and $18 per hour as a shift lead, and you work overtime in a week that includes both roles, the overtime rate is calculated from the blended hourly rate. Add up all regular earnings for the week and divide by total hours to find the blended rate. Then multiply by 0.5 and apply that premium to the overtime hours.</p>

<h2>One Quick Check You Should Do</h2>

<p>Pull your last three pay stubs when you have a few minutes. Add up your actual hours for each workweek and verify the overtime hours match what was paid. Payroll errors are more common than most people realize, and the correction process is straightforward once you have documentation.</p>
