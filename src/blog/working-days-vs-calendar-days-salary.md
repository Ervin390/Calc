<h1>Working Days vs Calendar Days: Salary Proration</h1>
<p class="post-meta">Published August 24, 2026 · 4 min read</p>

<p>When payroll departments process mid-month starts or terminations, they choose between two core math formulas: working days (260-day year) or calendar days (365-day year). Both methods convert annual salary into a daily rate, but payouts vary slightly.</p>

<p>Here is how both accounting methods work in real payroll systems.</p>

<h2>Method 1: 260 Working Days (US Standard)</h2>

<p>The 260 working days method assumes 5 working days per week across 52 weeks a year (52 x 5 = 260). Weekends are excluded from the daily rate denominator.</p>

<p>For an $85,000 annual salary:</p>

<ul>
    <li>Daily rate: $85,000 / 260 = <strong>$326.92 per workday</strong></li>
    <li>If you work 10 workdays in a partial month: 10 x $326.92 = <strong>$3,269.20</strong></li>
</ul>

<h2>Method 2: 365 Calendar Days</h2>

<p>The 365 calendar days method spreads your annual salary across every calendar day of the year, including weekends.</p>

<p>For the same $85,000 annual salary:</p>

<ul>
    <li>Daily rate: $85,000 / 365 = <strong>$232.88 per calendar day</strong></li>
    <li>If you are on payroll for 14 calendar days (including weekends): 14 x $232.88 = <strong>$3,260.32</strong></li>
</ul>

<h2>Which Method Do Most Companies Use?</h2>

<p>Over 85% of US corporate payroll software relies on the 260 working days standard. It aligns cleanly with 40-hour workweeks and overtime calculations under the Fair Labor Standards Act.</p>
