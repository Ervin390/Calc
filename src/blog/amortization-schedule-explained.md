<h1>Amortization Schedule Explained: Why Early Loan Payments Go Mostly to Interest</h1>
<p class="post-meta">Published {{ date | readableDate }} · 2 min read</p>

<p>Month one of your mortgage. You make a payment of $1,800. If you check where that money went, the breakdown might look something like this: $1,450 to interest, $350 to principal. You have owned the property for a full month and reduced your debt by less than $400.</p>

<p>This is not a mistake or a trick. It is the structure of an amortizing loan, and understanding it explains why the strategies for paying loans off early work the way they do.</p>

<h2>What Amortization Means</h2>

<p>Amortization is the process of paying down a debt through regular installments over a fixed term. Each payment covers the interest owed for that period plus a portion of the remaining principal. The total payment amount stays fixed for the life of the loan. What changes each month is how that fixed payment is divided between interest and principal.</p>

<h2>Why Interest Dominates Early Payments</h2>

<p>Interest is calculated as a percentage of the outstanding balance. At the start of a 30-year mortgage on a $300,000 loan at 6.5%, the outstanding balance is $300,000. Your first month's interest charge is 6.5% divided by 12 months, multiplied by $300,000, roughly $1,625.</p>

<p>After that payment, the principal has reduced slightly, so the next month's interest charge is fractionally smaller. That freed fraction gets added to the principal portion. Over time this shift accelerates, by year 25 of a 30-year loan, the majority of each payment is going to principal rather than interest.</p>

<h2>Reading Your Actual Amortization Schedule</h2>

<p>An amortization schedule is a table that lists every payment over the life of the loan. Each row shows the payment number, the payment date, the interest portion, the principal portion, and the remaining balance. Looking at your own schedule is the clearest way to understand where your money goes and to plan extra payment strategies effectively.</p>

<h2>The Strategic Insight Extra Payments Unlock</h2>

<p>When you make an extra payment and direct it to principal, you reduce the outstanding balance immediately. The following month's interest calculation is lower because it is charged against a smaller balance. Every extra principal payment compresses the interest portion of all future payments simultaneously. That is why early extra payments save disproportionately more interest than those made later in the loan term.</p>

<p>This is not a matter of paying more per month in the long run. A single $1,000 extra payment in year two of a 30-year loan at 6.5% eliminates approximately $3,700 in total interest over the remaining life of the loan. The math consistently surprises people when they see the actual figures.</p>

<h2>Fixed Rate vs Variable Rate Amortization</h2>

<p>Fixed rate loans amortize on a predictable schedule. The payment stays the same every month and the interest/principal split shifts gradually as described. Variable rate loans recalculate the payment periodically as the rate changes, which creates a new amortization curve each time the rate adjusts. The core dynamic of interest dominance early in the loan still applies, but the exact schedule cannot be projected far into the future.</p>
