<h1>Weighted vs Unweighted GPA: What Do Elite Colleges Actually Look For?</h1>
<p class="post-meta">Published {{ date | readableDate }} · 2 min read</p>

<p>This question comes up in every high school guidance counselor's office and most family kitchens at some point. The short answer is that it depends on the school, but the longer answer is more useful for actually getting into the institutions you are targeting.</p>

<h2>The Difference Between Weighted and Unweighted GPA</h2>

<p>An unweighted GPA treats every class identically, scoring A through F on a 4.0 scale regardless of whether the course is remedial, standard, honors, AP, or IB. A 4.0 unweighted GPA means you received an A in every class you took.</p>

<p>A weighted GPA adds extra points for more rigorous coursework. At most American high schools, honors courses add 0.5 points to the base value and AP or IB courses add 1.0 points. An A in an AP class scores as 5.0 rather than 4.0, which means weighted GPAs can exceed 4.0 significantly for students in demanding programs.</p>

<h2>What Admissions Officers Actually See</h2>

<p>Highly selective universities almost universally recalculate GPAs on their own internal scale when evaluating applicants. They convert your transcript into a standardized format that allows apples-to-apples comparisons across thousands of different high schools with different grading scales and weighting policies. The number you report on your application is context, not the final word.</p>

<p>What this means practically is that the two factors admissions offices weight most heavily are the rigor of your course selection and  your performance within that rigor. A 3.8 unweighted GPA across mostly standard courses reads very differently from a 3.8 unweighted GPA across a schedule packed with AP and dual enrollment classes.</p>

<h2>Why Course Rigor Matters as Much as the Number</h2>

<p>An admissions reader asking themselves whether to admit a student with a 3.7 GPA and eight AP courses is having a very different internal conversation than when reviewing a 3.9 GPA built on a light course load. Both GPAs are strong. One demonstrates willingness to challenge, which is a better predictor of college academic success than raw high school GPA alone.</p>

<p>This does not mean you should overload your schedule with rigorous classes you will perform poorly in. A 3.3 across AP classes is not at most schools evaluated more favorably than a 3.9 across standard classes. Balance is the actual goal, take the most challenging curriculum in which you can still perform at a high level.</p>

<h2>Which GPA to Report</h2>

<p>The Common Application asks for your unweighted GPA on a 4.0 scale. Most schools also ask for your school's reported GPA and the grading scale used, which gives them the context to interpret your number correctly. Report both if asked. Do not try to calculate and report a weighted GPA when an unweighted one is requested.</p>
