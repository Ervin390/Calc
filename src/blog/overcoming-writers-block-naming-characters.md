<h1>Top 5 Strategies for Overcoming Writer's Block When Naming Minor Characters</h1>
<p class="post-meta">Published {{ date | readableDate }} · 3 min read</p>

<p>Naming your protagonist takes time and feels important. Naming the shopkeeper who appears in two scenes and then never again should take thirty seconds. Somehow it often takes twenty minutes because the wrong name there still feels wrong.</p>

<p>Here are five approaches that consistently break the block and get you moving again.</p>

<h2>1. Generate a Batch and Pick From a List</h2>

<p>The problem with trying to invent a single name from nothing is that your brain cycles between a handful of familiar options. Expanding your options breaks the loop. Generate twenty or thirty names at once, skim them, and pick the one that feels right. You notice good candidates much more easily when you are comparing rather than inventing.</p>

<p>A random name generator is ideal for this. You can select male, female, or neutral names and generate lists of up to a hundred in one click. The cognitive load drops immediately when you are choosing rather than creating.</p>

<h2>2. Steal From Your Real Life Periphery</h2>

<p>Open your phone contacts. Look at names of people you barely know, old school acquaintances, service technicians, someone from a work event years ago. These are real names with the right phonetic weight but no emotional association for you as the writer. They tend to feel grounded rather than invented.</p>

<p>Mix and match, take a first name from one contact and a surname from a completely different one. The combination will be fresh even though both halves were sourced from reality.</p>

<h2>3. Match the Name to the Setting's Era and Geography</h2>

<p>A Victorian-era background character named Tyler immediately reads wrong. The mismatch pulls readers out. Spend thirty seconds thinking about where and when your story is set, then filter your search through that lens.</p>

<p>A medieval English setting calls for names like Edmund, Margery, or Hilda. A 1970s New York story works with names like Donna, Gary, or Keith. When the name fits the setting properly readers do not even consciously notice it, which is exactly what you want for a minor character.</p>

<h2>4. Use the Character's Function as a Name Clue</h2>

<p>Minor characters often exist to fill a specific narrative role. A gruff dock worker needs a name that sounds gruff. A fussy aristocrat needs something with enough syllables to sound self-important. Your intuitions about what sounds right for a role are usually correct, trust them and use them as a filter on whatever list you generate.</p>

<h2>5. Placeholder First, Refine Later</h2>

<p>If you have been stuck on one name for more than five minutes, type "NAMEX" and keep writing. Reserve one editing pass at the end of the draft specifically for filling in placeholders. You will find that once the character has had a few scenes, the right name often becomes obvious because you now know how they actually behave rather than how you imagined they would.</p>

<p>The goal of a first draft is to get the story down. A minor character's name matters, but it matters far less than finishing the chapter today.</p>
