<h1>What Is Time and a Half and How Is It Calculated</h1>
<p style="color:#6b7280; font-size:0.9rem;">Published August 9, 2026 · 5 min read</p>

<p>Time and a half sounds like it should be complicated but it really is not. You work more than your standard hours and your employer pays you 1.5 times your normal rate for those extra hours. That is it.</p>

<p>If you earn $18 per hour, your overtime rate is $27. Work five hours of overtime on top of your normal 40-hour week and those five hours pay $135 instead of $90. Your total paycheck goes up by $45 for the same five hours of work. Over a year, those extra earnings add up fast.</p>

<h2>Why the Law Requires It</h2>

<p>The Fair Labor Standards Act, passed in 1938, established the 40-hour workweek as the standard threshold. The law requires most employers to pay at least 1.5 times the regular rate for any hours above that. The goal was partly to discourage employers from overworking employees and partly to spread available jobs across more workers.</p>

<p>The FLSA does not cap how many overtime hours you can work. It just says those extra hours cost more. Some employers use this as a budget control, others embrace it when they need to staff up quickly during a busy period.</p>

<h2>Exactly How the Math Works</h2>

<p>The calculation has three steps.</p>

<p>First, find your overtime rate. Multiply your regular hourly rate by 1.5.</p>

<p>Second, calculate your regular pay. Multiply your standard hours by your regular rate.</p>

<p>Third, add your overtime. Multiply your overtime hours by the overtime rate and add it to your regular pay.</p>

<p>Say you earn $22 per hour and worked 47 hours last week. Your regular pay covers the first 40 hours: 40 times $22 equals $880. Your overtime rate is $33. Seven hours at $33 equals $231. Total gross pay for the week is $1,111.</p>

<h2>Who Qualifies for Overtime Pay</h2>

<p>Most hourly workers are covered. Salaried workers can also qualify depending on how much they earn. The current salary threshold under federal law is $684 per week or $35,568 per year. Employees earning below that amount are generally entitled to overtime regardless of their job title.</p>

<p>Employees earning above the threshold may still qualify if their duties do not meet the criteria for an executive, administrative, or professional exemption. The job title does not determine exemption status. The actual job duties do.</p>

<p>Some workers are fully exempt from overtime laws regardless of salary. These include certain agricultural workers, railroad employees, and some transportation workers covered by different federal regulations.</p>

<h2>State Rules That Go Further Than Federal Law</h2>

<p>Federal law sets the floor. States can go higher. California is the most notable example. California requires overtime for any hours worked beyond 8 in a single day, not just beyond 40 per week. Work 10 hours in one day and the last two hours are overtime even if your total for the week is still under 40. Work more than 12 hours in a single day and those hours past 12 are paid at double time.</p>

<p>Alaska, Nevada, and a few other states also have daily overtime rules. Check your state labor board website if you think your situation might qualify for daily overtime.</p>

<h2>What to Do If Your Employer Is Not Paying Overtime</h2>

<p>Wage theft through unpaid overtime is common and recoverable. The US Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division handles complaints and can require employers to pay back wages plus damages. You can file a complaint at dol.gov. You can also hire an employment attorney. Many take wage theft cases on contingency, meaning you only pay if you win.</p>

<p>Keep records of your hours. Clock-in and clock-out records, emails about scheduling, and any written schedules all help establish how many hours you actually worked.</p>
