<h1>Average Pay Raise Percentage by Industry in 2026</h1>
<p style="color:#6b7280; font-size:0.9rem;">Published August 9, 2026 · 8 min read</p>

<p>Before you walk into a salary review conversation, you need to know what the market is actually paying. Getting a 2% raise feels great if your industry average is 1.5%, and terrible if it is 6%. Context is everything, and the gap between the median and what top performers receive is wider than most employees realize.</p>

<p>Here is a comprehensive breakdown of what organizations across major sectors are budgeting for pay increases in 2026, based on compensation surveys from WorldatWork, Mercer, and Willis Towers Watson, plus specific guidance on how to position your own negotiation relative to these benchmarks.</p>

<h2>Overall Market Baseline for 2026</h2>

<p>The median salary budget increase across all US industries in 2026 sits at approximately 3.7%. That is slightly higher than the pre-pandemic baseline of 3.0% but has pulled back from the elevated 4.0% to 4.5% range seen during the 2021 to 2023 labor shortage period. Inflation cooling has given employers somewhat more breathing room, and the urgency to retain workers through large across-the-board increases has softened considerably compared to the previous three years.</p>

<p>However, the headline budget figure and what high-performing individuals actually receive are very different numbers. The budget pools the raises of everyone from the top performer who receives 6% to the bottom performer who receives 0% or a PIP. If you are a strong performer, the relevant benchmark is not the median; it is the 75th to 90th percentile of raise distribution in your sector.</p>

<h2>What These Numbers Actually Mean for Your Negotiation</h2>

<p>A raise at or below your industry median is essentially a maintenance increase. It keeps your salary nominally even with market movement but does not close any compression gap that may have opened since your last increase. If inflation ran at 3.2% over the past year and your raise is 3.5%, your actual real-dollar purchasing power increased by approximately 0.3 percentage points. That is not growth.</p>

<p>Top performers typically receive 1.5 to 2 times the median raise percentage within their company's budget pool. If your company's total salary budget is 3.7% and you receive 3.7%, you are being treated as a median contributor regardless of your actual performance rating. If the budget is 3.7% and you receive 5.5%, you are clearly in the high-performer tier and management has advocated for additional budget on your behalf.</p>

<p>Understanding this math matters because it shifts the goal of your negotiation. You are not trying to get a larger share of the industry average. You are arguing to be placed in the high-performer distribution within your specific organization's merit budget, which often exists as a separate pool from the standard COLA-linked increases.</p>

<h2>The Cost of Living Adjustment Is Not a Real Raise</h2>

<p>Many employees conflate their total raise percentage with actual salary growth. A 3.7% raise in a year when inflation ran at 3.5% represents a real-terms pay increase of approximately 0.2%. From a purchasing power standpoint, you are essentially treading water. You have more dollars, but those dollars buy almost the same amount they did before the raise.</p>

<p>A genuine raise in real terms means your salary is growing faster than inflation. Historically, this has required receiving around 5% or higher in years with moderate inflation, or securing a role change, promotion, or competing offer that justifies above-market compensation. Using our <a href="/hourly-to-salary-calculator/">hourly to salary calculator</a>, you can convert any proposed compensation to its annual equivalent and assess whether the total package represents real growth relative to where you currently stand.</p>

<h2>When to Negotiate Your Raise: Timing Strategy</h2>

<p>The most effective window for raise conversations is three to four months before your formal annual review date. That is when salary budget planning processes are actively running. Finance teams are building the numbers, managers are being asked what they need for their teams, and department heads are submitting requests. Walking into your review day and asking for a higher number puts you in a significantly weaker position because the budget allocations are usually already finalized and approved.</p>

<p>Making your case early, with specific and quantified examples of your contributions, gives your manager time to advocate upward through the chain of approval. This is especially true in larger organizations where individual line-manager authority on compensation is limited and must be approved by HR or finance before it is formalized.</p>

<h3>Steps to Prepare a Data-Driven Raise Conversation</h3>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Benchmark your market rate.</strong> Use Glassdoor, Levels.fyi (for tech), LinkedIn Salary, or Bureau of Labor Statistics data to find the 50th and 75th percentile for your role, location, and experience level. This is your anchor data.</li>
  <li><strong>List your measurable contributions.</strong> Revenue generated or influenced, cost savings delivered, projects led, team members mentored, systems built. Specific dollar figures are far more persuasive than adjectives.</li>
  <li><strong>Calculate the dollar impact of your raise request.</strong> Use a <a href="/pay-raise-calculator/">pay raise calculator</a> to convert your requested percentage to the exact annual and monthly dollar amounts. Showing you understand the cost signals seriousness and professionalism.</li>
  <li><strong>Identify your BATNA.</strong> Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. Do you have a competing offer? Are you actively interviewing? Negotiators with alternatives receive meaningfully higher outcomes. If you do not have one, consider creating one.</li>
  <li><strong>Prepare a specific ask, not a range.</strong> "I am requesting a 6% adjustment" outperforms "I was hoping for somewhere between 4% and 7%." Ranges anchor to their bottom, not their top.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Changing Jobs vs. Internal Raises: The Salary Jump Math</h2>

<p>Changing jobs remains the fastest path to a meaningful salary jump. Internal merit raises typically max out at 10% even for the highest-performing employees at most organizations, and that is the ceiling when everything goes perfectly. Competing offers from the external market routinely unlock 15% to 25% increases for equivalent or similar work.</p>

<p>The compounding effect of this gap is significant. An employee who receives 3.5% annually for five years has a cumulative raise of approximately 18.8% over that period. An employee who makes one strategic job move at a 20% increase and then accepts standard raises afterward has permanently shifted their salary baseline and will likely out-earn their peer by a substantial margin through the rest of their career.</p>

<p>This does not mean job-hopping every two years is always the right strategy. The non-monetary costs of a role change (relationship capital, learning curve, risk, benefits cliff) are real. But purely from a compensation optimization standpoint, the external market is significantly more generous than internal merit pools, and understanding this should inform how you prioritize your career decisions in any year where you are not receiving a raise that meaningfully outpaces the market.</p>
